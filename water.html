Water & Landform Processes

The Japanese Alps

The "Japanese Alps" is a common name for the Hida, Kiso, and Akaishi Mountains that bisect the main island of Honshu. These dramatic, steep mountain ranges dictate much of the country's terrestrial hydrology.

Because the mountains are so close to the sea, Japanese rivers are notoriously short, swift, and steep. Unlike the slow, meandering rivers of large continental plains, Japan's rivers plunge rapidly from high elevations down to the coast.

This topography results in immense kinetic energy in the river systems, leading to rapid fluvial erosion and deep V-shaped valleys.

Erosion & Coastal Plains

Weathering & Erosion

The combination of steep mountainous terrain, frequent tectonic tremors, and heavy precipitation (especially during typhoon season) makes Japan highly susceptible to extreme weathering and mass wasting events, such as landslides and mudflows.

Fluvial Processes

As the swift rivers reach the ocean, they suddenly lose their velocity, depositing massive amounts of sediment. Over millennia, this fluvial deposition has created the small but vital coastal plains where almost all of Japanese agriculture and urban development take place.

The Kanto Plain

The largest of these coastal plains is the Kanto Plain, home to Tokyo. Despite its size relative to the rest of the country, it only covers about 17,000 square kilometers—a testament to how scarce flat land is in the archipelago.
